\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

In this section, we describe systems that enable mobile devices to determine
their own position using nearby wireless infrastructure. Then, we define and
describe the identifiers used by one type of these systems.

\subsection{Positioning Systems}
\label{sec:positioning}

\emph{Wireless positioning systems} are used by mobile operating systems to
compute their own locations. These systems use landmarks---either \wifi \acp{AP}
or cellular base stations---with known locations to provide devices attempting
to self-geolocate with reference points to do so. \acp{WPS} use
\acp{BSSID}---MAC addresses of \wifi \acp{AP}---that are broadcast in 802.11
\wifi management frames to identify nearby landmarks, while \acp{CPS} use
identifiers broadcast by cellular \acp{BTS} to identify these landmarks.

While prior work~\cite{rye2024surveilling, han2022location,
tippenhauer2009attacks} examined the operation and privacy issues associated
with \acp{WPS}, we exclusively study the operation and privacy issues related to
\acp{CPS} in this work.

\subsection{Cellular Infrastructure Identifiers} 
\label{sec:bsids}

To advertise service for a cellular network, cellular \acp{BTS} advertise
identifiers over the air to clients within transmission range. These identifiers
indicate which \ac{MNO} the \ac{BTS} access to via the
\ac{PLMN} which consists of an \ac{ITU}-assigned \ac{MCC} and a
nationally-administered \ac{MNC}. Cellular infrastructure identifiers also
indicate a geographic area and unique identity of the \ac{BTS} or \ac{BTS}
sector. These two fields are assigned by \acp{MNO} to their cellular
infrastructure in a manner of their choosing.  When taken together, these four
values should uniquely identify a specific cell for a \ac{MNO}.

The name and size of the two fields assigned by an \ac{MNO} vary according to
the technology generation of the infrastructure they describe. For instance, in
\ac{GSM} (2G) and \ac{UMTS} (3G) networks, a \emph{Cell Global Identity (CGI)}
consists of a PLMN, a 16-bit regional identity called the \ac{LAC}, and a 16-bit
\ac{CID}. In \ac{LTE} (4G), an \emph{\ac{ECGI}} consists of a PLMN, a 20-bit
eNodeB ID, and an 8-bit \ac{CID}. In \ac{NR} (5G) networks, an \emph{\ac{NCGI}}
uniquely describes a cell, consisting of a PLMN, a 28-bit gNodeB ID, and an
8-bit \ac{CID}. Table~\ref{tab:cellids} summarizes the types of cellular
infrastructure identifiers and their sizes by technology generation.

In this work, we amass a global corpus of infrastructure identifiers across 2-5G
cellular technologies using Apple's \ac{CPS}. Because we are concerned with
identifying, geolocating, and longitudinally tracking these identifiers via a
\ac{CPS}, rather than transmitting or receiving these identifiers with our own
hardware, we are able to disregard many of the nuances in naming and bit-size
between technology generations. In this work, when the distinction between the
identifier used to specify a cell's location area or region is immaterial, we
refer to this identifier as a \ac{LAC}. We refer to the cell identifier,
irrespective of generation and bit-length, as the \ac{CID} throughout this work. 

\subsection{Apple's \ac{CPS}}
\label{sec:cps}

Lastly, we describe the operation of one \ac{CPS} in particular---that run by
Apple to support devices in its ecosystem. Apple's \ac{CPS} operates in two
distinct but concurrent phases. In the \emph{CPS data collection} phase, cellular
Apple devices (\eg iPhones, cellular-capable iPads) collect identifiers
advertised by nearby cellular \acp{BTS}. Periodically, these devices upload
the identifiers they observed, along with the locations they were at when they
observed them, the received signal strength of the observation, and other
metadata information. These reports populate the database of cellular landmarks
that Apple uses. Apple devices need not connect to a particular \ac{BTS} for
it to be used as a landmark, and they report cellular infrastructure for
\acp{MNO} they are not subscribers of.

In the \emph{CPS geolocation} phase, a device in Apple's ecosystem uses these
previously-collected cellular landmarks for geolocation. For an Apple device to
obtain its location using the \ac{CPS}, it samples the cellular frequency bands
for nearby identifiers, and queries a specific Apple API endpoint for this
purpose. Apple, if it has entries for the cellular identifiers requested by the
client in its database, will return the geolocations of those landmarks. Then,
using the received signals strengths from those nearby cellular landmarks, the
Apple device can estimate its own position. However, like Apple's \ac{WPS},
Apple's \ac{CPS} returns up to \textcolor{red}{how many??} additional
unrequested landmarks \emph{near} the landmarks that were requested.
